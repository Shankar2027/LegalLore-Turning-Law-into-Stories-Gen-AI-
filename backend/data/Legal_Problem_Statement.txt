Affidavit: A written promise, signed in front of an official, that what you are saying is the absolute truth.
Allegation: A claim that someone did something wrong, even though it hasn't been proven yet.
Amicus curiae: A friend of the court who isn't part of the case but offers helpful information to the judge.
Appeal: Asking a higher, more powerful judge to change a decision made by a lower judge.
Arbitration: A way to settle an argument where a neutral person makes a fair rule, instead of going to a real judge.
Arraignment: The first time someone goes to court to hear exactly what rules they are accused of breaking.
Bail: Money given to the court to let an arrested person go home until their trial, acting as a promise they will return.
Bench warrant: An order from a judge for the police to bring someone to court, usually because they missed a meeting.
Billable hours: The time a lawyer spends working for a client, which they charge money for.
Breach of contract: Breaking a serious, legal promise or agreement you made with someone else.
Burden of proof: The responsibility to show enough evidence that your side of the story is actually true.
Case law: Rules made by judges in past court cases that help decide future arguments.
Caveat: A warning or special rule to be careful about before agreeing to something.
Certiorari: An order from a very high court agreeing to look at the decision of a lower court to see if it was right.
Charge sheet: A formal list written by police that says exactly what rules a person is accused of breaking.
Civil litigation: A legal argument between two people or businesses, usually about money or property, not about going to jail.
Class action: A big lawsuit where a large group of people who were all hurt in the same way sue together as one team.
Closing argument: The final speech a lawyer gives at the end of a trial to summarize their side of the story.
Codicil: An extra page added to a will to change or update a few rules without rewriting the whole thing.
Common law: Traditional laws that come from old customs and past judge decisions rather than official rulebooks.
Complaint: The very first document filed in court that explains why someone is suing someone else.
Consideration: The thing of value, like money or an item, that people trade when they make a legal agreement.
Contempt of court: Getting in trouble for disrespecting the judge or refusing to follow court rules.
Contractual obligation: A strict duty to do exactly what you promised to do in a legal agreement.
Counterclaim: When someone is sued, but they turn around and sue the person who started it, saying they were the ones hurt instead.
Cross-examination: When a lawyer asks tough questions to a witness brought by the other side to see if they are telling the truth.
Damages: The money a court orders someone to pay to fix the harm or broken things they caused.
Decree: An official order or final decision made by a judge or leader.
Defendant: The person who is accused of breaking a rule or the person being sued in court.
Deposition: An interview under oath before a trial, where a witness answers questions while someone types every word.
Discovery: The time before a trial when both sides share information and clues so there are no surprises.
Docket: The court's official schedule or calendar of all the cases waiting to be heard.
Due diligence: Doing your homework and checking all the facts carefully before making a big deal or agreement.
Due process: The fair treatment every person deserves to get from the government and courts.
Easement: The legal right to cross or use a little piece of someone else's land for a specific reason.
Equity: Fairness in the law, where a judge makes sure everyone is treated right when strict rules don't fix the problem.
Estoppel: A rule that stops someone from changing their story if they already promised something different before.
Evidence: The clues, objects, and facts shown in court to prove what really happened.
Expert witness: A very smart specialist, like a doctor or scientist, who helps the court understand tricky facts.
Ex parte: When a judge talks to one side of an argument without the other side being there, usually for emergencies.
Exhibit: A specific piece of evidence, like a photo or a document, shown to the judge or jury during a trial.
Felony: A very serious crime that comes with harsh punishments.
Fiduciary duty: A very strong promise to put someone else's needs first and act with complete honesty.
Foreclosure: When a bank takes back a house because the person living there stopped paying for it.
Forensic evidence: Scientific clues collected from a crime scene, like fingerprints, used to solve a mystery.
Grand jury: A special group of people who listen to evidence secretly to decide if a trial should even happen.
Habeas corpus: A rule saying that if the police lock someone up, they must prove to a judge they have a good reason to do it.
Hearing: A meeting in court where a judge listens to arguments and evidence before making a decision.
Injunction: A strict court order telling someone they must stop doing something immediately.
Intellectual property: Ideas, inventions, or art created by your mind that belong to you, just like a physical toy would.
Interrogatories: A list of written questions one side sends to the other before a trial, which must be answered truthfully in writing.
Jurisdiction: The official power of a court to make decisions in a certain area or over a certain type of problem.
Jury: A group of everyday people chosen to listen to a trial and decide what the truth is.
Judgment: The final decision made by a court at the end of a trial.
Judicial review: The power of the highest courts to check if a law is fair and follows the constitution.
Jurisprudence: The study and science of what laws are, where they come from, and how they work.
Laches: A rule saying if you wait way too long to complain about something in court, you might lose your chance.
Legal precedent: An old court decision that acts as an example or rule for how future cases should be decided.
Liability: Being legally responsible for something, especially if it caused harm or costs money.
Litigation: The whole process of taking a disagreement to court and fighting it out legally.
Malpractice: When a professional, like a doctor or lawyer, makes a bad mistake that hurts someone they were supposed to help.
Mediation: Having a neutral peacemaker help two fighting sides talk and agree on a solution without going to trial.
Mens rea: The guilty mind or bad intention someone had when they decided to break a rule.
Merger clause: A rule in a contract saying that only the words written on the paper matter, ignoring past promises.
Misrepresentation: Telling a lie or hiding an important truth to trick someone into agreeing to something.
Motion: A formal request asking a judge to make a specific order or decision during a case.
Negligence: Being very careless and accidentally causing harm to someone else because you didn't pay attention.
Non-disclosure agreement (NDA): A written promise to keep a secret and not share certain information with anyone else.
Notary public: A trusted official who checks people's IDs and watches them sign important papers to prove it is real.
Objection: When a lawyer stands up to say the other side is breaking a courtroom rule and asks the judge to stop them.
Opinion: The written explanation from a judge detailing why they made a specific decision.
Ordinance: A local law or rule made by a city or town, like how loud music can be.
Paralegal: A person who is trained to know the law and help lawyers prepare for cases, but is not a lawyer themselves.
Patent: A special document from the government that says you invented something new and no one else can copy it.
Perjury: Committing the serious crime of lying while you are under oath to tell the truth in court.
Plaintiff: The person who starts a lawsuit by complaining that someone else hurt them or broke a rule.
Plea bargain: An agreement where an accused person admits they did something wrong to get a smaller punishment instead of risking a big trial.
Power of attorney: A legal paper that gives someone else the power to make important choices or sign documents for you.
Precedent: A past action or decision that serves as a guide for what to do in the same situation later.
Prima facie: Something that looks true right away when you first look at it, unless someone proves otherwise.
Probate: The official court process of checking if a dead person's will is real and sharing their things fairly.
Pro bono: When a lawyer does their work for free to help someone who doesn't have much money.
Prosecution: The team of lawyers who work for the government trying to prove that someone committed a crime.
Public defender: A lawyer paid by the government to help an accused person who cannot afford to hire their own lawyer.
Quash: When a judge completely cancels an order or throws out a piece of evidence.
Reasonable doubt: The feeling of not being completely sure someone is guilty, which means they shouldn't be punished.
Rebuttal: Presenting evidence or arguments to prove that what the other side just said is wrong.
Recusal: When a judge steps away from a case because they have a connection to it and want to make sure the trial is fair.
Remedy: The solution a court orders to fix a problem, like paying money to replace something broken.
Res judicata: A rule that says once a case has been completely decided, you can't go back to court and fight about the exact same thing again.
Retainer: An upfront fee paid to a lawyer to make sure they are available to help you when you need them.
Settlement: An agreement to end an argument and close a case before a judge has to make a final decision.
Statute: A formal, written law that was voted on and passed by the government.
Statute of limitations: A countdown clock that says you only have a certain amount of time to start a lawsuit after something happens.
Subpoena: An official written order from a court that commands a person to show up and tell what they know.
Summary judgment: A quick decision made by a judge without a full trial because the facts are so obvious that one side clearly wins.
Surety: A person who takes responsibility for someone else's promise, like promising to pay if the other person doesn't.
Testimony: The spoken evidence a witness gives in court after promising to tell the truth.
Tort: A harmful action that hurts someone or damages their stuff, allowing the hurt person to sue for money.
Trademark: A special logo, word, or symbol that shows exactly what company made a product so it can't be copied.
Trial: The main event in a courtroom where evidence is shown and a judge or jury decides who is right.
Trust: A legal arrangement where one person safely holds money or property for the benefit of someone else.
Unlawful detainer: The legal process a landlord uses to force a tenant to leave a house if they refuse to pay rent.
Verdict: The final answer given by a jury at the end of a trial, deciding if someone is guilty or not.
Voir dire: The questioning process where lawyers pick fair and honest people to serve on a jury.
Waiver: A document you sign to officially give up a right or agree not to sue if you get hurt doing something fun but risky.
Warrant: A piece of paper signed by a judge giving police the power to arrest someone or search their house.
Will: A legal document you write to tell people who should get your belongings after you pass away.
Writ: A formal written command from a court telling someone to do something or stop doing something.
Zero-hour contract: A job agreement where the boss doesn't promise any specific amount of work, and the worker only gets paid for the hours they actually do.
Corporate governance: The rules and practices by which a company is directed and controlled, like the rulebook for running a big business.
Shareholder agreement: A special contract between the people who own pieces of a company, explaining what they can and cannot do.
Articles of incorporation: The official birth certificate of a company, filed with the government to make it real.
Compliance: Following all the laws, rules, and regulations that apply to a business or activity.
Regulatory framework: The complete system of rules and guidelines that an industry must follow to stay out of trouble.
Antitrust law: Rules designed to stop huge companies from becoming monopolies and cheating their customers by preventing competition.
Insider trading: The illegal act of buying or selling stocks because you know a secret about the company that the public doesn't know yet.
Due diligence report: A deep-dive research document explaining all the risks and facts about a company before someone buys it.
Legal audit: A complete checkup by lawyers to make sure a company is following all the rules and hasn't made any illegal mistakes.
Contract enforcement: Using the power of the law to make sure everyone actually does what they promised to do in an agreement.
Data protection law: Strict rules that force companies to keep your personal information, like passwords and addresses, completely safe.
